Toward World Models That Extrapolate Indefinitely

The past, present, and future of streaming video generation

From Video Generation to Real-Time Interaction

In an interactive world, the future unfolds with each new input. Turn left, and the model needs to reveal what lies to the left. Keep moving, and generation must keep up. That requires a basic capability: producing what comes next, on time, from what has already happened.

CausVid 10 was an important early step in this direction. Bidirectional video diffusion jointly denoises an entire clip, allowing each frame to draw on future information. Causal generation instead moves forward through time, predicting the present from the past. CausVid distills a bidirectional teacher into a few-step causal student and reuses past computation through a KV cache, bringing high-quality video models closer to streaming output.

Efficient autoregressive generation involves two distinct questions. How should training organize past and present content so the model learns to continue a sequence? And how can each prediction require less denoising computation, so generation can keep pace with interaction?

Teacher Forcing trains the model to predict from ground-truth history. Diffusion Forcing 8 allows different sequence positions to have different noise levels, bringing sequence prediction and diffusion denoising into a common framework. Few-step distillation addresses the computational side: sequential output is useful for interaction only if each step arrives quickly enough.

Streaming video turns generation into a repeated prediction process. Every prediction creates the conditions for the next. This exposes a mismatch between training and inference. Teacher Forcing supplies history from real videos; autoregressive inference conditions on the model's own outputs. Even small errors enter the context for later predictions and can accumulate as generation continues.

Learning to predict from real history therefore does not guarantee that a model can continue from its own outputs. Self Forcing 2 brings self-generated history into training. The model performs autoregressive rollouts with a KV cache and learns through video-level distribution matching. Training now exposes it to the deviations its own outputs introduce, so it can learn to continue under those conditions.

Better Quality, Longer Horizons

After Self Forcing, several distinct bottlenecks come into focus: how to initialize the student, how to supervise long rollouts through short windows, and how to support longer inference with limited context.

Where should the student start? Causal Forcing 11 addresses initialization. During ODE initialization of a few-step model, a bidirectional teacher can use future information that the causal student cannot access. A target that depends on this hidden information can assign different answers to the same visible input. Causal Forcing uses an autoregressive teacher for ODE initialization, aligning the targets with the information available to the student, before proceeding to Self Forcing distribution matching.

Causal Forcing++ 12 advances framewise generation in one or two steps. Its causal consistency distillation uses online teacher supervision at adjacent timesteps to learn a few-step initialization, reducing the need to precompute and store full ODE trajectories. Fewer sampling steps and finer output granularity both reduce the wait during interaction.

A better initialization improves the starting point. Longer generation also requires bridging the gap between the training window and the inference horizon. Extending a training window means more spatiotemporal tokens and intermediate states, increasing both memory use and computation. Bidirectional teachers, meanwhile, can reliably supervise only short clips. A real interaction may last minutes or hours—far beyond the interval that training directly covers.

How can short-window supervision support longer rollouts? LongLive 4 uses streaming long tuning to continue segment by segment along the model's own history, with a short-video teacher supervising each segment. Short-window attention and a frame sink control cost and retain historical anchors. Self-Forcing++ 5 instead samples clips from long self-generated videos for distillation. Both approaches break a long trajectory into pieces the teacher can assess, extending the reach of local supervision.

Rolling Forcing 13 changes how generation advances. It jointly denoises multiple frames at different noise levels, relaxing strict causality between neighboring frames. How far generation can go also depends on how each step is organized.

How can limited context support longer inference? As a rollout grows, retaining and reading its entire history becomes impractical. The model must select useful information within a fixed cache budget. Deep Forcing 6 preserves early history as an anchor and uses attention from recent predictions to select historical KV entries, reducing redundancy. Infinity-RoPE 14 addresses temporal position encoding: a moving local coordinate system organizes past and present content without letting time indices grow beyond the model's original positional range. These approaches improve context use through history selection and position encoding, respectively.

Each direction acts on a different part of the process. Initialization sets the student's starting point. Long-rollout training and denoising schedules shape how generation advances. History selection determines what survives in context, while position encoding establishes its temporal relationship to the current prediction.

Let Future Losses Teach the Model What to Remember

Initialization, long-rollout training, history selection, and position encoding have all improved autoregressive video generation. Yet beyond the training window, errors can still accumulate into visual drift and breakdown. One possible source lies in how history is represented: the model learns to use historical representations, while the process that constructs them still lacks supervision.

Past frames must be encoded into KV representations before later predictions can read them. Learning to use those representations is only part of the task; the model also needs to learn how to construct them. Since history serves future prediction, future generation losses can flow back through the historical KV and teach the model to retain more useful information.

In Self Forcing, gradients are stopped along the history-to-KV construction path, preventing later losses from updating that computation. Self Gradient Forcing (SGF) 3 restores these context gradients, allowing future losses to supervise the construction of historical representations.

SGF splits training into two passes to avoid backpropagating through the full sampling trajectory. The first runs a rollout without gradients, recording generated latents and noisy inputs at the sampled denoising exit. The second performs parallel context-gradient reconstruction under a causal mask. Sampled latents remain detached, while the reconstructed KV path is differentiable.

SGF learns to build useful history within a short training window, then repeatedly applies that writing mechanism during longer inference. It substantially improves long-horizon consistency when the inference horizon exceeds the training window.

Restoring history-writing gradients also revealed something unexpected: in some examples, SGF's first frame was slightly blurrier than Self Forcing's. The first frame has no historical video KV to read, making this change a useful clue. Could the gradients that learn historical representations interfere with those that denoise the current frame?

Self Gradient Forcing Plus (SGF+) 1 measures these two gradient contributions separately in Wan2.1 15. In both Attention and FFN, the paired gradients have negative cosine similarity, indicating conflicting updates to the shared parameters. Adding the two contributions partially cancels them, making it harder to optimize both roles together.

A similar pattern appears in LTX-2.5 16. In the heatmaps below, mean paired gradient cosine similarity is negative for most layer–noise-level combinations, with conflict strength varying across layers and noise levels. These measurements provide further evidence of gradient conflict between context writing and denoising.

SGF+ responds with role-specific parameterization: the context writer and denoiser receive separate parameters. The writer encodes clean history into KV; the denoiser reads those KV representations to recover the current frame. The two cooperate through the historical representations and train jointly under the same generation objective.

This separation preserves the supervision path restored by SGF. The loss updates the denoiser directly and reaches the writer through the denoiser's dependence on historical KV. The writer can therefore learn to support future generation while having its own parameter space. A shared objective connects the two roles, while separate parameters let each optimize its own computation.

Across framewise and chunkwise settings, SGF+ improves visual quality and long-horizon consistency. With a five-second training window, it demonstrates continuous video generation lasting up to 24 hours, without long-video fine-tuning. The result illustrates how far the inference horizon can extend beyond the training window.

SGF and SGF+ bring two related questions into training: how historical representations receive supervision, and how parameters are allocated between roles. SGF lets future losses train history writing. SGF+ addresses the resulting conflict with current-frame denoising. Both ask how a model can leave behind a more useful past.

Some Further Thoughts

History writing and current-frame denoising are closely connected through KV, but that connection does not require identical weights. Separating the roles opens several directions worth exploring.

The connection to GPT and LLMs. Both are autoregressive, so why do GPT-style LLMs typically share parameters across these roles, while video models may benefit from separating them? Language understanding and generation are widely modeled through a common token-prediction framework; unifying visual understanding and generation remains more open-ended. Computationally, GPT builds KV and predicts the next token in a single forward pass. Autoregressive video diffusion alternates between context writing at t=0 and denoising at t>0. Context writing extracts historical information for future predictions; denoising recovers the current frame from a noisy input. These different input states and computational roles may place different—even conflicting—demands on shared parameters.

Parameter separation could start earlier. Differences and negative alignment between role gradients are already visible in the TF-initialized model. The conflict does not first appear after SGF training. Separation could therefore begin during TF initialization, letting the model learn history construction and history-conditioned prediction as distinct roles from the outset. The same idea could extend to World Action Models, where Teacher Forcing is a common training approach. Separating context writing from visual and action prediction may help models learn historical representations that better serve subsequent predictions.

Lightweight writers and KV compression. Separate roles allow capacity to be allocated according to need: context writing and denoising need not use equally large networks. One direction is a lightweight writer that compresses redundant video history into useful KV representations under a limited cache budget. During training, the denoiser's parameters could stay fixed while future generation losses optimize the writer through its KV outputs. The aim is to preserve visual quality and long-horizon consistency while reducing parameter storage, KV-cache memory, and attention costs.

From Long Videos to Interactive Worlds

Training windows are finite. Interactions with a world have no predetermined endpoint. This raises a longer-term question: can a model learn generation and memory mechanisms from brief training sequences, then reuse them far into the future?

SGF and SGF+ explore one concrete route. Historical representations support future predictions, and future losses can in turn teach the model how to construct those representations. A model learns to retain useful information within a short window, then repeatedly applies that ability during longer inference. The length of the training window need not directly determine how long inference can continue.

Moving from long videos to interactive worlds requires extending this ability to actions, state, and changes in the environment. A user acts, the model generates a consequence, and that consequence enters the history for the next interaction. As this cycle repeats, spatial relationships, object identities, and the consequences of actions need to remain consistent.

This suggests a direction for future systems: let history writing preserve key state, let generation respond to new inputs, and train their cooperation through a shared objective. The vision of indefinite extrapolation is to use finite training and computation to support worlds that keep unfolding.
