How Should Autoregressive Video Models Learn from History?

Zihan Su, Tsinghua University

Autoregressive video models generate one frame or chunk at a time, conditioning each step on what came before. As generation continues, the model must preserve character appearance, scene structure, and coherent motion. This depends on how it represents the history and how it uses those representations.

From ground-truth history to self-generated history

A natural way to train an autoregressive model is to provide ground-truth context frames from a training video and ask it to predict what comes next. This is Teacher Forcing (TF): the model denoises the current input conditioned on those clean, ground-truth frames.

\[ \hat{x}_i=\mathcal D_\theta\!\left(z_i^t,t\mid x_{<i}^{\mathrm{data}}\right),\qquad x_{<i}^{\mathrm{data}}\sim p_{\mathrm{data}}. \tag{1} \]

Here, \(z_i^t\) is the noisy representation of the current frame, \(\hat{x}_i\) is the predicted clean frame, and \(x_{<i}^{\mathrm{data}}\) is a history drawn from the video data distribution \(p_{\mathrm{data}}\).

Ground-truth sequences provide readily available supervision and allow predictions at multiple positions to be trained in parallel with causal attention. The model can learn relationships between past and future frames without first generating the whole video step by step. At inference, however, its own outputs may look different from those ground-truth frames.

Suppose an object changes shape early in a generated video. Every prediction that follows must work with that altered object. If the training sequences never contain this kind of error, the model may not know how to continue. Further errors can compound the initial mistake, carrying generation increasingly far from familiar training conditions.

Self Forcing (SF) 2 trains the model on histories it generates itself. The student rolls out a video autoregressively, conditioning each prediction on its previous outputs, and trains with a distribution matching distillation (DMD) objective. It therefore learns on the states it actually encounters during generation, with an opportunity to adjust its predictions when its own outputs go wrong.

\[ \hat{x}_i=\mathcal D_\theta\!\left(z_i^t,t\mid\tilde{x}_{<i}\right),\qquad \tilde{x}_{<i}\sim p_\theta. \tag{2} \]

Here, \(\tilde{x}_{<i}\) is the history generated by the current model, and \(p_\theta\) denotes its generation distribution. The current frame is now predicted from self-generated history rather than ground-truth history.

SF can nevertheless accumulate substantial errors beyond the training window. Because each prediction depends on earlier outputs, an early deviation can influence the rest of the video. Over time, this can lead to abrupt scene changes, deformed objects, disappearing subjects, or additional people appearing.

Reducing error accumulation requires a closer look at how the model records and uses its history. Expanding Eq. (2) to show both prediction and KV construction gives:

\[ \hat{x}_i = \mathcal D_{\theta}\!\left( z_i^t,\,t \;\middle|\; \mathrm{KV}_{<i} \right), \qquad i=1,\ldots,T,\quad t>0. \tag{3} \]
\[ \mathrm{KV}_{i} = \mathcal D_{\theta}\!\left( \tilde{x}_i,\,t_{\mathrm{ctx}} \;\middle|\; \mathrm{KV}_{<i} \right),\qquad t_{\mathrm{ctx}}=0. \tag{4} \]

To predict the current frame, \(\mathcal D_\theta\) reads \(\mathrm{KV}_{<i}\) and denoises \(z_i^t\) to produce the clean-frame estimate \(\hat{x}_i\). To write the history, the same model encodes the generated frame \(\tilde{x}_i\) at timestep \(t_{\mathrm{ctx}}=0\), producing \(\mathrm{KV}_i\) for later predictions. \(T\) is the training window length.

A direct response is to increase \(T\) and generate longer videos during training. LongLive 4 extends each rollout one segment at a time, using a short-video teacher to provide DMD supervision for every new segment. Self-Forcing++ 5 distills contiguous clips sampled from longer self-generated sequences. These methods expose the model to degraded states that short rollouts rarely reach, allowing it to learn how to continue when errors are already present.

Other methods improve inference by selecting which entries in \(\mathrm{KV}_{<i}\) remain available to the current prediction. Deep Forcing 6 keeps a substantial portion of early history as a reference, then uses attention scores from recent predictions to select cache entries. Keeping useful entries and removing redundant ones makes better use of a limited cache during long generation.

These methods primarily address Eq. (3), improving long-horizon generation through longer training sequences or historical KV selection. Equation (4) leaves room for further exploration: What should the KV representation of history encode to help future predictions? In SF, gradients stop at the written KV, so future losses cannot train the computation that produced it. Extending the rollout or changing the cache policy does not, on its own, reopen that gradient path.

Letting future predictions supervise historical representations

Self Gradient Forcing (SGF) 3 takes Eq. (4) as its starting point. It allows future prediction losses to train context writing, so the model learns to encode past frames in ways that support later predictions.

In the notation of Eq. (4), SF and SGF differ in where they stop the gradient:

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathrm{SF}:\quad \mathrm{KV}_i &=\operatorname{sg}\!\left( \mathcal D_\theta(\tilde{x}_i,t_{\mathrm{ctx}}\mid\mathrm{KV}_{<i}) \right),\\ \mathrm{SGF}:\quad \mathrm{KV}_i &=\mathcal D_\theta\!\left( \operatorname{sg}(\tilde{x}_i),t_{\mathrm{ctx}}\mid\mathrm{KV}_{<i} \right). \end{aligned} \tag{5} \]

Here, \(\operatorname{sg}\) denotes stop-gradient. SF treats the written KV as a fixed condition. SGF instead detaches the generated frames while keeping the reconstructed KV differentiable.

SGF implements this with two passes. The first rolls out a video autoregressively without gradients, recording the generated frames and the noisy inputs at selected denoising steps. The second reuses those records to reconstruct the KV cache and target predictions in parallel under causal attention. The generated frames stay detached, while KV construction remains differentiable. Gradients from later predictions can then flow through the KV to train how earlier frames are encoded, without backpropagating through the entire sequential sampling trajectory.

The loss on the prediction in Eq. (3) can now train context writing in Eq. (4). The input frames need not change: the model can learn to retain different information, or encode it differently in KV, according to what later predictions need.

When context writing and denoising conflict

Restoring context-writing gradients improves long-horizon consistency, yet some SGF samples have a slightly blurrier first frame than SF samples. This is revealing because the first frame has no historical video KV to read. Could the gradients that teach the model to represent history interfere with those that teach it to denoise the current frame?

In SGF, denoising in Eq. (3) and context writing in Eq. (4) share parameters \(\theta\). One recovers the current frame from noisy input. The other encodes clean past frames for future predictions. Although both serve the same generation objective, they may push the shared parameters in different directions.

The two gradient contributions can be isolated within a single reconstruction pass. Let \(g_{\mathrm C}\) denote the contribution from context writing and \(g_{\mathrm D}\) the contribution from denoising. Their sum is the gradient applied to the shared parameters, and their cosine similarity measures directional alignment:

\[ g_\theta=g_{\mathrm C}+g_{\mathrm D}, \qquad s=\frac{\langle g_{\mathrm C},g_{\mathrm D}\rangle} {\lVert g_{\mathrm C}\rVert_2\lVert g_{\mathrm D}\rVert_2}. \tag{6} \]

When \(s<0\), the gradients form an angle greater than \(90^\circ\) and partially cancel when combined. Measurements across 128 prompts and four denoising timesteps give 512 gradient pairs for each of the Attention and FFN module families. Every pair has negative cosine similarity, indicating conflict across all measured prompts and timesteps. The t-SNE projection also shows distinct clusters for the two roles. The mean gradient directions form angles of approximately \(104.2^\circ\) in Attention and \(106.3^\circ\) in FFN.

These measurements reveal a gradient conflict between context writing and denoising. SGF restores supervision for context writing, but the two contributions still act on shared weights and partially cancel. The interference makes it harder to learn useful context representations and improve current-frame denoising at the same time.

Self Gradient Forcing Plus (SGF+) 1 therefore assigns separate parameters to the two roles. The denoiser \(\mathcal D_{\theta_d}\) generates the current frame, while the context writer \(\mathcal C_{\theta_c}\) builds historical KV for future predictions. Equations (3) and (4) become:

\[ \begin{aligned} \hat{x}_i &=\mathcal D_{\theta_d}\!\left(z_i^t,t\mid\mathrm{KV}_{<i}\right), \qquad t>0,\\ \mathrm{KV}_i &=\mathcal C_{\theta_c}\!\left( \operatorname{sg}(\tilde{x}_i),t_{\mathrm{ctx}}\mid\mathrm{KV}_{<i} \right), \qquad t_{\mathrm{ctx}}=0. \end{aligned} \tag{7} \]

With separate parameters, the two roles still learn together through KV. The prediction loss updates the denoiser and backpropagates through its KV inputs to train the context writer. The writer learns what to retain for later generation, but its gradient no longer competes with the denoising gradient on the same weights.

The training procedure still uses two passes and the original DMD objective, with no additional memory loss. The denoiser learns to generate the current frame from its context, while the writer learns to prepare that context for future predictions. Each now has its own parameters to update.

TF and SF differ in the histories used for training. SGF allows future predictions to supervise how those histories are encoded. SGF+ then separates the parameters used to encode history from those used to denoise the current frame, accommodating the different updates each role requires.

What role separation suggests

Connections to GPT and LLMs. LLMs also generate autoregressively. Why do they not need role separation, while video models need to consider it? Language understanding and generation are widely modeled with a single token-prediction objective. An equivalent framework for visual understanding and generation remains an open question. GPT constructs KV and predicts the next token in one forward pass. Autoregressive video diffusion alternates between encoding clean context at \(t=0\) and denoising at \(t>0\). Context writing extracts information for future predictions, whereas denoising recovers the current frame from noisy input. The different input conditions and computational roles may place different, even conflicting, demands on shared parameters.

Parameter separation can begin earlier. Role gradients already differ and exhibit negative alignment at TF initialization, before SGF training begins. This suggests introducing separate parameters during TF itself, allowing context encoding and prediction to develop separately from the outset. World action models commonly use Teacher Forcing and offer a natural setting for this approach. Separating context writing from visual and action prediction could help them learn more useful representations of history for later predictions.

Lightweight writers and KV compression. The writer and denoiser need not have the same capacity. A lightweight writer could learn to compress redundant video history into KV representations that fit within a limited cache budget. It could be trained through future generation losses while keeping the denoiser's parameters and its method of reading KV fixed. The aim is to reduce parameter storage, cache memory, and attention cost without sacrificing generation quality or long-horizon consistency.

Conclusion

TF trains on ground-truth histories. SF moves training onto histories generated by the model itself, so it can learn to continue from its own outputs. SGF makes context writing differentiable, allowing future prediction losses to train the representation of those histories. SGF+ gives context writing and denoising separate parameters to address their conflicting gradients. Both roles still learn from the same generation objective.

How should autoregressive video models learn from history? They must learn both to work with their own outputs and to encode those outputs in a form that helps future predictions. Training needs to address how the model reads history as well as how it writes it, so that both roles can learn effectively from the same generation objective.
